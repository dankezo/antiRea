# v2.6.2 deep dive

## Summary

v2.6.2 is a patch release with one theme: clock discipline (audit item L-DEEP-2). A full audit of the shipped v2.6.1 `src/` tree classified every clock read by what the value means. Twelve points measured an INTERVAL - a window, a TTL, a decay age, a refill elapsed time - by subtracting two wall-clock (`Date.now`) reads, and every such interval inherits any wall-clock step: an NTP correction, a VM suspend/resume or a manual clock change. A lab harness running the shipped 2.6.1 modules proved the distortion in both directions: a +5 min step let one identity take 200 records in 10 real seconds against a 100-record window budget (200%), and a -5 min step kept a spent window closed for an extra 300 s. The patch moves all twelve interval points to a monotonic clock (`performance.now`, via a new internal module `src/internal/mono-clock.ts`), with a hybrid two-deadline design for the two persisted ledgers. There are no new features and no breaking changes: the public-contract impact is additive only (one new optional `monoNow` option per patched module); the existing `now` contract is unchanged, and a host that injects only `now` keeps the pre-patch single-clock behavior exactly. The CSPRNG half of the audit found nothing to fix: `Math.random` appears 0 times in `src/`. Suite: 322 tests (320 pass, 0 fail, 2 pre-existing RSA-PSS skips), up from 305 in v2.6.1, with 17 new regression tests and zero pre-existing tests modified.

## 1. The wall-clock problem

### How a clock step breaks an interval

Wall clocks step. NTP daemons correct drift in jumps, hypervisors suspend and resume VMs, and administrators set the time by hand. Any computation of the form `now - then >= windowMs` silently absorbs the jump: a forward step makes every stored timestamp look older than it is (windows "expire" early, TTLs lapse, evidence decays), and a backward step makes them look younger (windows outlive their design, nonces stay "live" past their TTL). Nothing logs an error; the arithmetic is correct and the inputs are wrong. An anti-abuse layer is exactly the wrong place for this failure mode, because its windows and budgets ARE the product: a step that resets a budget window early is a free refill for whoever is extracting at that moment, and a step that stretches a window is a denial of service against legitimate users who happen to be inside it.

### Harness proof (shipped 2.6.1 modules)

The audit built a lab harness (`l-deep-2-lab/`) around verbatim copies of the shipped 2.6.1 `dist` modules for the two window guards, with the ONLY difference between branches being the injected clock source: wall = epoch + real elapsed + a step offset (simulating `Date.now` across an NTP step), mono = a step-free source (simulating `performance.now`). Configuration: 60,000 ms window, budget 100 records (record budget) / 30 requests (quota guard), step +/-300,000 ms (5 minutes). Time is virtual, so runs are byte-identical across repetitions (Node v24.22.1).

- **Scenario 1, +5 min step, record budget:** an identity spends 100/100 at t=0; 10 real seconds pass; the +300 s step lands; the identity asks for a fresh batch of 100. Wall branch: the pre-check read the window as expired, the batch was counted as a brand-new window - **200 distinct records served in 10 real seconds = 200% of the window budget**. Mono branch: `allowed = false`, the batch counted against the same window (`used = 200`, `exceeded = true`); the budget held at 100 per window.
- **Scenario 2, -5 min step, record budget:** an identity spends 100/100; the -300 s step lands; state is read at real second 70, when the 60 s window should already have reset at second 60. Wall branch: `used = 100, remaining = 0` - the window only resets at real second **360**, so the block lasts **exactly 300 s longer than designed**. Mono branch: `used = 0, remaining = 100` - reset on schedule.
- **Scenario 3, +5 min step, quota guard sliding window:** 30 requests (the anonymous tier ceiling) at t=0; the step lands; a second burst of 30 arrives 1 real second later; the guard's decision input is `peek().count`. Wall branch: `count = 30` - the first burst was pruned as "older than 60 s", the window forgot it entirely. Mono branch: `count = 60` - both bursts remembered, the guard sees 2x the ceiling.

The token-bucket refill family (CUSUM pacing, both ledgers) was not given its own harness: the arithmetic is the same shape, and the code already carries two partial guards (`Math.max(0, elapsed)` against backward steps, burst caps against forward steps), so the audited impact there is bounded at "bucket fills early" rather than a forgotten or doubled window. That sentence is code analysis, not a measurement, and is labeled as such.

### Classification

Every `Date.now` / `new Date` / `performance.now` / `hrtime` / randomness read in the 29-file `src/` tree was traced to the arithmetic that consumes it and classified by the MEANING of the value, not by variable names:

- **(a) Interval points - must move to monotonic: 12.** Ten are purely in-memory (both ends of the subtraction are produced by the same process and the state lives in RAM). Two are hybrid: their state is persisted and shared across processes, so monotonic values cannot simply replace the stored wall timestamps (section 3).
- **(b) Randomness that must move to a CSPRNG: 0.** `Math.random` appears 0 times in `src/`. The security randomness that exists was already CSPRNG: the canary-echo nonce is 128 bits from Node `crypto.randomBytes(16)`, and the transport shield draws its AES-GCM IV and nonce from `webcrypto.getRandomValues`. Two deterministic mulberry32 PRNGs (Canary Maze layout, decoy document generation) are intentional design - the same path must always produce the same maze/decoy - and are classified as keep, with the recorded boundary condition that they must never be reused to mint ids or tokens; grep confirms no call site does.
- **(c) Wall-clock points that stay: 11 groups**, each with a stated reason (section 4).

## 2. The monotonic fix

### The anchor: monoNowMs

New internal file `src/internal/mono-clock.ts` (not exported from `src/index.ts`). `monoNowMs()` returns `performance.now()` shifted by an anchor captured once at module load: `MONO_ANCHOR_MS = Date.now() - performance.now()`. The anchor puts monotonic reads on roughly the same scale as `Date.now()` - handy when the two are compared in logs - while every DIFFERENCE between two monotonic reads is step-free by construction. The anchor is a scale convenience only; no semantics depend on it, and nothing persisted ever stores a monotonic value. `createIntervalClock()` returns the split default pair: `{ wall: () => Date.now(), mono: monoNowMs }`.

### The resolution rule (resolveIntervalClock)

Every patched module accepts an additive optional `monoNow?: () => number` option next to the existing `now`. `resolveIntervalClock({ now, monoNow })` encodes one rule:

- A host that injects `monoNow` drives the interval domain explicitly.
- A host that injects `now` but NOT `monoNow` keeps the pre-patch single-clock behavior exactly: the injected clock drives BOTH domains, so existing host clocks and test clocks behave bit-for-bit as they always have.
- A host that injects neither gets the split default: wall for display timestamps, calendar semantics and absolute deadlines; monotonic for every interval.

This rule is why the patch could land with zero pre-existing tests modified and zero broken: every test that injects a fake `now` sees identical semantics in both domains. Four modules (herd detector, action cohort, CUSUM tripwire, evidence attribution) plus the two ledgers spell the same rule inline (`options.monoNow ?? (options.now ? wall : monoNowMs)`) instead of importing the helper - same rule, different form. The transport shield's replay guard uses `options.monoNow ?? monoNowMs`, since its envelope timestamps are host-facing wall values with no injected-clock interplay. The honeypot also gained the clock-injection option it previously lacked entirely (it called `Date.now()` directly); its single `now` variable fed both the per-IP interval and the UTC calendar-day budget, so the patch splits it into two sources.

## 3. The two hybrid ledgers

### Earliest of two deadlines

The disclosure ledger and the action budget persist their state through a host store and share it across processes. Monotonic time is per-process and resets on restart, so it cannot be written into that state - a restored reservation has no meaningful monotonic birth time. The persisted state therefore keeps wall-clock deadlines exactly as before (`expiresAtMs`, `windowStartMs`, `lastRefillMs`), and the patch layers a process-local mirror on top instead: a factory-side map (`ownReservationMono` in both ledgers) records, for each reservation THIS process created, its monotonic creation stamp and TTL. A reservation created by this process dies at WHICHEVER COMES FIRST: its wall expiry or its monotonic TTL. A reservation restored from the store, or written by another process, has no mirror entry and follows the wall deadline alone - stated plainly in `docs/LIMITATIONS.md`, not hidden.

### Clamped refill and window anchors

The second mechanism bounds what a step can do to the shared arithmetic itself. Both ledgers compute bucket refills from elapsed wall time, but the credited elapsed is now clamped into `[0, msToFull]`, where `msToFull` is the time the bucket needs to refill completely at its configured rate: with a healthy clock the clamp is a mathematical no-op (a refill was already capped at the burst size), and under a step it converts an implicit behavior into an explicit bound - a forward step can never credit more time than a full bucket, and a backward step can never credit negative time. The action budget carries the same idea for windows: `windowAnchors` records the wall start and monotonic start of each subject/cohort window this process created or rolled, so such a window also rolls at its monotonic length even if the wall clock steps; an anchor is invalidated the moment the stored `windowStartMs` no longer matches it (another process rolled the window), after which the window is wall-governed until this process rolls it again.

## 4. What stays on the wall clock

Eleven groups keep wall-clock reads, by design, each because the value's meaning is absolute or cross-party rather than an in-process interval:

- **Signed-payload expiry:** cursor and cursor-v2 `iat`/`exp`, canary-echo ids - absolute deadlines embedded in payloads that another process or instance may verify later; epoch is the only time domain that means anything across processes. Their TTLs are bounded (300 s for cursors), capping any step's effect at the TTL.
- **Operator-issued validity windows:** calibration-manifest `issuedAtMs`/`expiresAtMs` and protected-cohort declaration `expiresAtMs`/`declaredAtMs` - an artifact's or a declaration's business validity is an absolute statement ("valid until time X"), not an interval this process measures.
- **Cross-machine protocol epochs:** RFC 9421 Web Bot Auth `created`/`expires` are epoch seconds issued by the SIGNER's machine; verification must compare in the wall domain (a `clockSkewSeconds` tolerance already exists). The transport envelope `ts` is likewise an epoch exchanged between two parties and bound into the AAD; its expiry check is a cross-party comparison with skew tolerance. (Only the replay guard's internal TTL bookkeeping moved - A9.)
- **Calendar semantics:** the Canary Maze daily cap is a UTC calendar-day rule (`toISOString().slice(0, 10)`); CUSUM epoch rotation and the herd/action formation windows run over host-supplied event timestamps.
- **Display and provenance:** ISO timestamps in logs, alerts, snapshots and the evidence reviewer's `generatedAtMs` exist for a human reader; wall clock is the correct domain, and none of them feed an interval computation.
- **Content, not time:** the decoy mirage's fake `effectiveFrom` date is generated document content.
- **Host-supplied timestamps:** event `ts` values the host attaches are the host's time domain; the SDK's monotonic clock cannot repair a host that supplies skewed timestamps. The SDK owns only its own `?? now()` fallbacks, which are counted in A4-A6. This is a coverage boundary, recorded so nobody reads the patch as "all time distortion is fixed".

## 5. Test evidence

- **Suite:** `npm run check` - 305 tests (303 pass) → **322 tests (320 pass, 0 fail, 2 pre-existing RSA-PSS skips)**. The delta is `tests/mono-clock.test.cjs`: 17 regression tests, zero pre-existing tests modified, zero broken - the resolution rule (section 2) is the reason: a test injecting only `now` drives both domains and sees pre-patch semantics exactly.
- **The 17 tests** replay the lab harness scenarios and semantics (60 s window, ceiling 100, +/-300 s steps) against the shipped modules: record budget under a +5 min step (window does not reset) and a -5 min step (window does not stretch); quota sliding window under a +5 min step (first burst not forgotten); flow-scorer idle under a -5 min step; herd co-activity on the monotonic clock; action-cohort canary taint expiry on the monotonic clock; disclosure-ledger reservation death at its monotonic TTL under a -5 min step; action-budget window roll on the monotonic clock under a -5 min step; CUSUM bucket refill in both step directions; Canary Maze per-IP window in both directions; evidence-attribution decay in both directions; replay-guard nonce TTL in both directions; behavior-shield route windows aging on `performance.now`.
- **Pre-patch evidence:** the lab harness of section 1 ran against verbatim copies of the shipped 2.6.1 `dist` modules, twice, with byte-identical output - 200 records in 10 real seconds (200% of budget) on the wall branch vs a held budget on the mono branch; a spent window unblocking at real second 360 instead of 60 on the wall branch vs on-schedule on the mono branch; quota `count` 30 (forgotten burst) vs 60.

## 6. Remaining limits and what this patch does not change

- **Restored and cross-process state is wall-only.** Persisted ledger/budget state carries wall deadlines only; a reservation or window restored from a store, or written by another process, has no monotonic mirror and follows the wall clock alone until this process creates or rolls its own. The mirrors are process-local maps, never persisted or shared. This is the honest edge of the design: monotonic time cannot be shared, and pretending otherwise would corrupt the ledgers' cross-process contract.
- **Monotonic time is per-process.** `performance.now()` resolution and suspend behavior are the platform's; the anchor is a scale convenience only, and no claim is made about intervals spanning a process restart.
- **Host clocks are out of scope.** Host-supplied event timestamps and host-provided custom stores run on the host's clock discipline; the patch removes distortion from the SDK's OWN clock reads only.
- **No behavior change for well-behaved hosts:** budgets, ceilings, pacing, cohort law, calibration defaults and every shipped constant are exactly as in v2.6.1. A host that injects its own `now` clock and no `monoNow` observes literally the pre-patch behavior.
- **The queued audit findings are NOT fixed here:** the remaining MEDIUM/LOW findings from the 2.6.0 audit (F-02 to F-05, F-08 to F-11 and the rest) keep their v2.6.1 behavior and stay queued for a later round, exactly as the 2.6.1 patch left them.
- **LIMITATIONS bookkeeping:** `docs/LIMITATIONS.md` gains exactly one row, "Clock discipline (L-DEEP-2)", stating what moved, the hybrid rule, and the cross-process boundary above. No measured-results number in the README changes: the harness numbers are a pre-patch defect demonstration, not a protection-effectiveness claim.
